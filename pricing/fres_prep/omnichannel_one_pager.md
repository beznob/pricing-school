One Price Across All Channels for FX Cash

Background

Most major UK travel money providers price online and in branch differently. The reason is cost: branch cash needs staff time, secure transport, and insurance. Also,  a study of large US retail chains found that keeping nearly uniform prices across stores carries a measurable cost (DellaVigna and Gentzkow, QJE 2019). When JCPenney moved to a single everyday price nationwide in 2012 without testing, sales fell roughly 25% over the following year.

Recommendation

A full move to one omnichannel price is almost irreversible: once we advertise one fair rate, going back would be a damaging public story. Its effects would also stay hidden, because after a national rollout, we could no longer see what would have happened without the move. We therefore recommend moving ahead step by step, testing before each step.

Approach

In terms of data, what we need is around three years of transactions with the margin on each, Finance's costs model for every channel with the method behind it, and competitor rates. Variable cost differs most between channels and sets the floor. Branch cost can be very variable, so we use a range rather than one figure. From the same data we estimate price sensitivity for each channel and currency, using a model of past rate changes that separates our effect from season, market swings and competitor moves. We also track competitor rates per currency: the best online providers and high-street prices. Any new rate must stay inside that range, and the tracking tells us whether a result came from our change or a competitor's move.

We test by region: change prices in some regions, leave similar ones untouched, and compare. Below are the recommended steps:

Design the test. A simulation rehearses the test on old transaction data: it adds the expected lift and checks the test would notice it. If it misses too often, we add regions or weeks. 

One rate across regions. Remove regional price differences in a few pairs of regions with similar past rates and volumes, far enough apart that customers do not cross.

Narrow the gap between online and branch. Change both channels in a region at once and judge its total profit. A loss means we adjust the rate or keep the gap.

Simulate the national rollout. A second simulation turns the test results into a range of profit/loss outcomes with uncertainty intervals.

Governance and final sign-off. An independent team checks the models and results (model risk management) before any national move.

Annex: the data I would ask for on day one

**Transaction history.** Two to three years of individual transactions from the data team: date, channel, branch and region, currency, amount, the rate we gave and the rate we bought at, so each transaction shows its margin. This is the first ask, because it builds the margin map, shows seasonality and gives every regional test its baseline.

**Variable cost by channel, with the method behind it.** From Finance: staff time per branch transaction, secure transport, insurance, the cost of holding cash in hundreds of branches, and for online the delivery, payment and fraud costs. It also covers the commission paid to the Post Office network on branch sales, since for FRES that is part of the true cost of the branch channel. Branch cost is an allocation across everything a counter sells, so I want the method, not just the number, and any decision uses a range.

**Competitor rates.** Daily rates per currency for the main online providers and the high street. Any test price must stay inside the market range, and the feed tells us whether a result came from our change or a competitor's move. If this feed does not exist yet, building it takes weeks, so it is a task, not a blocker.

**Channel overlap.** Click and collect volumes, the share of customers who use both channels, and use of the online rate checker if we track it. The main risk in narrowing the gap between online and branch is that customers switching channels look like growth, and we cannot judge that without knowing how much overlap already exists.

**Test design inputs.** The list of regions or branch groups with their size, the normal week-to-week swing in each region's sales, and a calendar of past marketing and operational events. The simulation uses the first two to decide how many regions and weeks a test needs; the calendar keeps a test away from weeks disturbed by other activity.

Whatever is missing or unreliable, we do not wait for it. We use a stand-in figure, state the assumption, and carry the uncertainty into the simulation as a range. The data pack decides how confident the first test can be, not whether we start.
