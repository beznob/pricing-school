# 5B · Causal Inference Primer — Why price changes don't mean what they seem

## The trap: correlation masquerading as causation

You raised your margin last summer. Volume went **up**. Does this mean higher 
prices attract customers? Of course not — but the data alone can't tell you that.

This file teaches the **six core concepts** that separate "what happened" 
(observed data) from "what would happen if you changed price, holding everything 
else still" (causal effect). We use small numbers, plain English, and the same 
travel-money business as the rest of the school.

---

## 1. Causal effect — the definition that settles everything

**Causal effect** is the counterfactual: the change in outcome *because* of 
a change in a treatment, holding all else constant.

Formally: "What is the difference between the world where we raised margin 
and the world where we didn't, *if all other factors stayed the same*?"

### Example: summer pricing

You run a travel-money desk. On June 15, you raise your margin from 2.0pp to 2.5pp.

**What you observe:**
- June 14 (2.0pp margin): 500 customers, average order $100 → $50,000 revenue
- June 15 (2.5pp margin): 600 customers, average order $100 → $150,000 revenue

**The naive conclusion:** Higher prices attract customers!  
**The truth you're missing:** June 15 was peak-season peak. Demand would have 
surged anyway. You don't know if price pushed volume up, down, or sideways.

**The causal effect** is the difference between:
- (Volume if we had raised price, in a world where June is still June)
- (Volume if we had left price alone, same June)

You never observe both worlds. That's why causal inference is hard.

---

## 2. Confounder — the culprit hiding in plain sight

A **confounder** is a variable that:
1. **Causes changes in the treatment** (price), AND
2. **Directly affects the outcome** (volume)

When both happen, you can't separate the treatment's effect from the confounder's effect.

### Example: the desk's pricing rule

Suppose your pricing desk follows this rule:
> Margin = 0.8 + 0.5 × (competitor's margin) + 0.3 × (season strength) + noise

**Confounders here:**
- **Competitor's margin** — when they go expensive, you go expensive too, AND 
  customers naturally buy more from you (they have fewer alternatives)
- **Season** — in peak season you raise prices (you can afford to), AND volume 
  is naturally high anyway (demand is strong)

Both push your margin *up* and your volume *up* simultaneously. If you fit a 
regression of volume against your margin, the slope will capture:
- Your true price effect (negative: higher price → less volume)
- PLUS the confounder effect (positive: competitor expensive + peak season → high volume)

The net result: the regression might show a *positive* slope even though your 
true elasticity is negative. **The confounder hides the truth.**

### How to spot a confounder

Ask: "Why did the price change?" If the answer includes anything that also 
pushes on demand, that's a confounder.

| Confounder | Why it changed price | How it affects demand directly |
|---|---|---|
| Competitor price | We react to them | Customers choose based on the gap |
| Season | Peak season justifies higher price | Peak season = high demand anyway |
| Fuel cost surge | Our costs rose | Customers cut travel spending |
| Vehicle fleet age | Old fleet is expensive to maintain | Older fleets crash more |

---

## 3. Endogeneity — the statistical name for "confounded"

**Endogeneity** means the treatment (price) is correlated with the error term 
in a regression — i.e., unobserved factors in the noise are pushing both price 
and outcome in the same direction.

In plain English: *your data was generated by a world where prices were set 
because of demand conditions*, not randomly assigned.

### Why it breaks regression

Regression assumes: "Move price, and *only* price changes; everything else is 
noise."

In reality: "When I see a high price, it's usually because conditions were 
favorable (competitor expensive, peak season). The high volume I observe isn't 
caused by the high price; it's caused by the favorable conditions."

**Result:** The regression slope is **biased** — it's measuring the desk's 
pricing rule, not the elasticity.

---

## 4. Exogenous variable — the opposite of endogenous

**Exogenous** means a variable whose value is determined *outside the system* 
you're studying. It is not influenced by the outcome.

In contrast, an **endogenous** variable is determined *within the system* — 
it could be influenced by the outcome, or affected by confounders that also 
influence the outcome.

### Example: weather

On a given day, the weather is **exogenous** to your travel-money sales 
(weather happened, then your customers came). But the weather affects demand:
- Rainy day → fewer people travel → lower sales
- Sunny day → more people travel → higher sales

If your pricing desk raises margins on sunny days (when they "can"), then 
weather becomes a confounder. The regression will blame the margin raise for 
the high sales, when weather did the work.

### Example: a regulatory change

A regulatory change (e.g. "starting Jan 1, all FX spreads must be < 2pp") is 
exogenous — you didn't choose it because of market conditions; it was imposed. 
This can be used as a **natural experiment** to estimate elasticity.

---

## 5. Control variable — holding the confounder still

A **control variable** (or **covariate**) is a confounder you put into your 
regression to **hold it constant** while you measure the treatment effect.

If you include competitor margin and season in the regression alongside your 
margin, you're asking: "What if competitor margin and season were the same 
two days in a row, but only our margin changed? How much would volume move?"

This isolates your price effect from the confounders' effects.

### The logic: residual-on-residual

Here's the intuition (and the deep version of this is called the 
**Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem**):

1. **Regress your margin on the confounders** → predict what your margin 
   "should" be given competitor and season → keep the leftovers (residuals), 
   the part of your margin the confounders *can't* explain
2. **Regress volume on the confounders** → predict what volume "should" be 
   given competitor and season → keep the leftovers
3. **Regress leftover volume on leftover margin** → the slope is your causal effect

Why? Because the residuals are the "free variation" — price and volume movements 
that have nothing to do with the confounders. Regressing them isolates the 
causal link.

### Example: the travel-money desk

**Raw data (confounded):**
| Day | Margin | Competitor | Season | Volume |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| June 1 | 2.0pp | 1.8pp | Peak | 600 |
| June 2 | 2.2pp | 1.9pp | Peak | 680 |
| Jan 1 | 1.5pp | 1.4pp | Low | 200 |
| Jan 2 | 1.6pp | 1.5pp | Low | 180 |

**Naive regression** (margin only): positive slope — high price, high volume.

**Controlled regression** (margin + competitor + season): negative slope — 
holding season and competitor constant, higher margin → lower volume.

---

## 6. Instrumental variable — randomness to the rescue

An **instrumental variable (IV)** is an exogenous variable that:
1. **Affects the treatment** (e.g., policy that restricts your pricing)
2. **Does NOT directly affect the outcome** (e.g., the policy didn't change demand, 
   only the constraint on your prices)

An IV lets you estimate causal effects even when you can't observe (or aren't 
aware of) all the confounders.

### Example: a price cap

Suppose regulators impose a **price cap of 2.0pp** on your margin, effective 
immediately. Before the cap, you could price up to 3.0pp on peak days. After, 
you can't.

**Before:** You set margin = 2.8pp (peak season). Volume = 700.  
**After:** You set margin = 2.0pp (capped). Volume = 400.

**Question:** What is your price elasticity?

**The naive answer:** (400 − 700) / 700 = -43%. Raise price 1%, lose 43% volume.  
**The problem:** You didn't *choose* to raise prices. The cap forced you lower. 
The change is contaminated by the fact that peak season is ending (confounded again!).

**The IV approach:** The cap is exogenous — you didn't choose it because of 
demand, and it didn't directly change demand (people still want euros as much 
as before). It *forced* you to change price. So:

*Elasticity ≈ (% change in volume) / (% change in price forced by the cap)*

This isolates the price effect because the IV breaks the link between demand 
conditions and your pricing decision.

**Why it's powerful:** You don't need to measure all the confounders. The IV 
does the work of breaking the feedback loop.

**Why it's weak:** The IV must be exogenous (really imposed, not chosen). If 
regulators tightened the cap in response to soaring demand, the IV is contaminated.

---

## 7. Selection bias — confounders you can't even name

**Selection bias** occurs when the group receiving the treatment (high price) 
differs systematically from the group not receiving it (low price) in ways 
that affect the outcome.

In the travel-money example: if you raise margins at the airport counter but 
not online, the two channels are inherently different:
- Airport: captive customers, less price-sensitive
- Online: comparison-site adjacent, more price-sensitive

The high margin at the airport "looks" like it has no effect (or even positive 
effect), not because airport customers don't care about price, but because the 
airport is already priced higher for unrelated reasons. You're comparing apples 
(captive) to oranges (price-sensitive).

**How to fix it:**
- **Randomize** the treatment (randomly choose which days to raise price)
- **Match** on observable confounders (compare only days with similar competitor 
  prices and seasons)
- **Stratify** (divide into strata of similar confounders, measure effect within 
  each stratum, then pool)

---

## 8. Causal inference in three methods (simple to strong)

### Method 1: Control for all confounders you can measure

**Assumption:** You've measured all the confounders. Unmeasured ones don't exist, 
or are small.

**Reality:** You probably missed some. But it's the most practical starting point.

**Example:** Regression of volume on (margin, competitor margin, season).

**Strength:** Simple, interpretable, checks feel "fair."  
**Weakness:** Unmeasured confounders still bias the result. The desk might have 
raised prices when forecasts predicted high demand (not yet visible in realized 
demand), or when vehicle fleet age was cheap (unrecorded).

---

### Method 2: Use an instrumental variable

**Assumption:** You have access to an exogenous shock (regulatory change, 
natural experiment, random event) that affects treatment but not outcome directly.

**Example:** A regulator imposes a price cap, or your company's system goes 
down for a day (random event), or a competitor's system goes down (their prices 
become unresponsive temporarily).

**Strength:** Doesn't require measuring confounders.  
**Weakness:** The IV must truly be exogenous (hard to verify). And it estimates 
the effect on the subgroup whose behavior the IV changes — not everyone (called 
the "local average treatment effect" or LATE).

---

### Method 3: Run a randomized experiment

**Assumption:** You can randomly assign treatment (price) and control groups 
(no treatment) and observe both.

**Example:** Randomly choose 50% of branches to raise margin to 2.5pp, keep 50% 
at 2.0pp, measure volume in both groups. A coin flip is exogenous — correlated 
with nothing.

**Strength:** Breaks all confounding. Confounders, unmeasured or not, are 
balanced between groups by randomization.  
**Weakness:** Can't always run experiments (can't randomize competitor prices, 
seasons, regulation). Takes time. Requires holdout groups.

---

## 9. Back to the travel-money desk: the four estimates

### Scenario

Your desk's data:
- 730 days of transaction-level history
- Three channels: Airport, High Street, Online
- Three elasticities (you set them, they're hidden): β = −0.3 (Airport), −0.8 
  (High Street), −1.5 (Online)
- Desk's rule: margin = 0.8 + 0.5 × competitor_margin + 0.3 × peak_season

### The four estimates you can compute

| Method | Airport estimate | High Street | Online | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Naive** (margin only) | +0.15 | −0.04 | −0.22 | Confounded; truth is −0.3, −0.8, −1.5 |
| **Controlled** (+ competitor + season) | −0.29 | −0.83 | −1.54 | Almost perfect; unmeasured confounders are small |
| **IV** (e.g., regulatory shock) | −0.31 | −0.79 | −1.49 | Exogenous variation; assumes IV is truly exogenous |
| **Experiment** (random price test) | −0.30 | −0.78 | −1.51 | Randomization breaks all confounding |

## 10. The hierarchy: when to use which method

1. **Start with control variables.** They're simple and cheap. If controlled 
   and uncontrolled estimates agree, you're probably not missing big confounders.
2. **If they disagree, hunt for unmeasured confounders.** Was there a fleet 
   change? A marketing push? A holiday promotion?
3. **Run a small experiment** to validate. Even 100 branches, two weeks, is enough 
   to rule out major confounder bias.
4. **Use an IV if randomization is impossible** (can't randomize competitor 
   prices, weather, season). But verify the IV is truly exogenous.
5. **Deploy with monitoring.** Track out-of-time data. If new elasticity estimates 
   diverge from your lab estimate, a confounder or a market shift has arrived.

## 11. One more trap: colliders

A **collider** is the opposite of a confounder. It's a variable that is 
*caused by both* the treatment and the outcome, but doesn't cause either.

### Example: complaint volume

Suppose high prices cause fewer complaints (fewer sales, fewer issues), and 
high demand causes more complaints (more transactions). Complaint volume is 
caused by both price and demand, so it's a collider.

**If you control for complaint volume** (include it in your regression), you 
accidentally induce bias. You're "explaining away" the effect of price on 
demand by conditioning on the consequences, which opens up a spurious path 
between price and volume through complaint volume.

**Rule:** Don't control for variables that are consequences of the treatment. 
Only control for confounders (causes of both treatment and outcome) and 
adjustment for sampling (e.g., stratification).

## 12. The econometrics corner — how the pros write it down

*(Absorbed from the former handbook chapter on elasticity estimation.)*

**The log-log workhorse.** Regressing `log(Q)` on `log(P)` gives the elasticity
directly as the slope (`d log Q / d log P = ε`); constant-elasticity demand is
`Q = A·P^ε`. Only valid if `log P` is exogenous — which is the whole point of
this file.

**Arc vs point elasticity.** Arc uses midpoints
(`ΔQ/avg(Q) ÷ ΔP/avg(P)`) and is symmetric to the direction of change — use it
for two observed price points. Point elasticity is the derivative — use it on a
fitted curve.

**Cross-price elasticity.** `ε_xy = %ΔQ_x / %ΔP_y`. Positive → substitutes (a
rival's price rise helps you); negative → complements. This is how you quantify
a competitor move in £ (File 19 §5).

**2SLS mechanics (the IV recipe, honestly):**
1. Stage 1: regress price on the instrument(s) + controls → fitted price `P̂`.
   **Report the first-stage F — you need F > 10** (ideally much more); a weak
   instrument gives estimates that are biased and wildly variable — worse than OLS.
2. Stage 2: regress `log Q` on `P̂` + controls; the coefficient on `P̂` is the
   causal elasticity. Use proper 2SLS standard errors, not naive stage-2 ones.
3. Classic pricing instruments: **cost shocks** (wholesale FX cost, fuel, input
   prices), regulated caps, competitor supply shifts, Hausman instruments
   (same product's price in other markets).
4. **Compare OLS vs IV — a big gap is expected and is the story.** In the case
   study, pooled OLS returns a *positive* +0.40 while IV recovers the true ≈ −1.9.

**The direction of the bias:** firms move price *with* demand, so naive OLS
makes demand look more inelastic than it is (or upward-sloping) → pricing on it
means systematic over-charging and quiet volume bleed.

**Heterogeneity is where the money is.** One pooled number hides the prize —
estimate per segment/channel/corridor (segmented IV), and at scale with double
ML / causal forests (§13). Targeted pricing on heterogeneous elasticity is how
uniform value-destruction becomes targeted value-creation.

## 13. The methods catalogue — the full causal toolkit

*(Absorbed from the former handbook chapter on causal inference &
experimentation. The ladder of evidence, most to least credible: randomised
experiment → natural experiment → IV → selection-on-observables → raw
correlation.)*

**Units of randomisation for price experiments:**
- **Customer-level A/B** — cleanest statistically, but live price discrimination
  on identical products is a conduct/PR risk in regulated retail.
- **Geo experiments** — randomise by region/store; avoids within-market
  discrimination; fewer units → less power (File 06 + notebook N1).
- **Switchback / time-sliced** — flip the whole market on a schedule; handle
  autocorrelation and carryover.
- **Segment-level** — move a whole segment vs a matched holdout.

Design essentials (File 06): power/MDE up front, a permanent holdout, guardrail
metrics (complaints, churn, fair-value flags — not just revenue), full demand
cycles, spillover containment.

**Quasi-experimental methods (when you can't randomise):**

| Method | When | Key assumption |
|---|---|---|
| **Difference-in-differences** | A change hits some units, not others | **Parallel trends** — check pre-trends |
| **Regression discontinuity (RDD)** | Price/eligibility flips at a threshold | Units just above/below the cutoff are comparable; estimates a *local* effect |
| **Synthetic control** | One treated unit, several controls | A weighted blend of controls reconstructs the pre-trend (File 08 §5's matched twin, formalised) |
| **Event study** | A discrete price/competitor event | No confounding event in the window |

**ML for causal pricing:**
- **Double / debiased ML (DML)** — the §5 residual-on-residual trick with ML in
  steps 1–2 (cross-fitted, Neyman-orthogonal): rich controls without
  overfitting bias.
- **Causal forests / GRF** — heterogeneous treatment effects (CATE):
  customer-level elasticity as a function of features.
- **Uplift modelling** — model the *incremental* response to a discount; target
  the persuadables, not the sure-things or lost causes.

**Online learning (bandits):** each price is an arm; ε-greedy (simple),
**Thompson sampling** (explores in proportion to uncertainty), contextual
bandits (learn a pricing *policy*). All of it belongs inside guardrails —
caps/floors/rate-limits, non-stationarity handling, fairness and
competition-law constraints (Files 12 §5, 19 §4).

**Validity checklist:** internal (is assignment really independent of demand?
pre-trends, first-stage F, balance) · external (does the test generalise to
rollout?) · interference/SUTVA (do treated units contaminate controls?) ·
power (can it see a commercially meaningful effect?) · conduct (is
differential live pricing defensible?).

## 14. Quick reference — the cheat sheet

**The one-page picture:**

```
CONFOUNDER (e.g., peak season)
    ↙              ↘
  YOUR PRICE    VOLUME SOLD
```
Regression sees price ↔ volume and calls it causality; both were driven by the season.

**Decision tree:**
1. Can you randomise? → do it (breaks all confounding).
2. No, but a natural experiment / exogenous shock exists? → use it as an IV.
3. Neither → control for every confounder you can measure, then compare
   controlled vs naive: agreement is comfort; a big gap means a missing confounder.

**Common confounders in pricing:**

| Decision | Likely confounder |
|---|---|
| Raise margin in Q2 | Peak season |
| Raise when competitor is expensive | Competitor price |
| Raise when inventory is low | Stock level / scarcity demand |
| Raise for new customers | Segment risk profile |
| Raise when costs spike | Cost changes |

**Red flags for unmeasured confounding:** naive and controlled estimates differ
a lot · a positive price coefficient · the estimate jumps when you add a
control · the sign flips between observational data and an experiment (trust
the experiment).

**One-liners to remember:**
- Naive elasticity = my pricing rule, not customer demand.
- Control variables hold measured confounders still; randomisation handles the unmeasured ones.
- Don't control for colliders (consequences, not causes).
- A corner-solution price ("raise to the cap") is a warning, not an answer.
- If naive and controlled differ a lot, you're missing something.

## 15. Glossary of causal terms

| Term | Plain English | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Causal effect** | Difference in outcome because of treatment, holding all else still | What you want to measure |
| **Confounder** | A cause of both treatment and outcome; hides the treatment's true effect | Enemy |
| **Endogeneity** | Statistical term for "confounded": treatment correlated with error term | The problem that blocks regression |
| **Exogenous** | Determined outside the system; doesn't respond to outcome | Friend; use as control or IV |
| **Control variable** | A confounder you put into regression to hold it constant | Weapon against endogeneity |
| **Instrumental variable** | Exogenous shock that changes treatment but not outcome directly | Weapon when you can't measure confounders |
| **Selection bias** | Treatment and control groups differ in unmeasured ways | Threat in observational data |
| **Randomization** | Assigning treatment via coin flip | Perfect defense against all confounding |
| **Collider** | A consequence of both treatment and outcome; don't control for it | Trap |
| **LATE** | "Local average treatment effect" — IV estimates effect on subgroup whose behavior the IV changes | What IV actually measures |

## Practice

1. You raise your margin in Q2 (peak season). Revenue goes up. Is this a causal 
   effect of the price rise? What confounder(s) might be hiding?

2. You run a regression: log(volume) ~ margin + competitor_margin. You find 
   the margin coefficient is −0.5. Is this the true elasticity? What would 
   change if you added "season" as a control?

3. A regulator caps your margin at 1.5pp. You observe volume rise 20% after the cap. 
   Is this a valid estimate of elasticity? Why or why not?

4. Your data shows: high complaint volume → high prices (desk raises prices when 
   complaints spike to avoid liability). Should you control for complaints in 
   your elasticity model? Why?

---

## Answers

1. **Not clear.** Q2 is peak season, and peak season has high demand *independent 
   of* your price. The confounder is **season**. You can't tell from the data 
   alone whether the price rise caused the revenue increase or if the season did. 
   You'd control for season (and competitor price) to isolate the price effect.

2. **Probably yes, if you've measured the right confounders.** Competitor margin 
   and season are the two big ones from your desk's rule. If you've controlled 
   for them, −0.5 is close to unbiased. But unmeasured confounders (fuel costs, 
   fleet age, promotions) could still bias it. The controlled version should be 
   *closer* to the truth than the naive (margin-only) version.

3. **Not directly.** The cap is exogenous (imposed, not chosen), so it's a valid 
   instrument. But the coefficient is (% volume change) / (% price change forced 
   by the cap). If the cap is binding and your elasticity is constant, this 
   works. **However:** If the cap is 1.5pp and your unconstrained margin would 
   have been 1.2pp, the cap didn't force a change — you weren't constrained anyway. 
   The IV is weak.

4. **No.** Complaints are a *consequence* of high prices (fewer sales, fewer 
   complaints). They are a collider. Controlling for them opens a spurious path 
   and induces bias. Control for **causes** of price and volume (confounders), 
   not consequences.
